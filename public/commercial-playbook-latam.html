1. Why LatAm Revenue Stalls — and Why It's Not the Product

Every international expansion goes through two critical stages. The first is product-market fit in the new region: proving that local buyers have the problem your product solves. The second is GTM fit: proving you can sell there in a repeatable and scalable way.

Most companies that come to us have already passed the first stage. Inbound leads arrive from Brazil and Mexico, a few customers signed remotely, maybe a reseller is active. But LatAm revenue has been flat for 6, 9, 12 months. The wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong solution: hiring more local salespeople without a process scales the chaos; hiring a country manager without architecture results in ~50% failure in the first year.

The problem is rarely the product. In most cases: a sales process copied from headquarters without local qualification criteria, an ICP too broad for the region, every deal escalated to HQ for approval, or a pipeline full of opportunities that never close.

Situational Triggers — When Timing Accelerates the Decision

Beyond the chronic signals above, there are trigger situations that make the moment urgent:

  • The country manager or regional sales head left — the region has no revenue leadership and someone at HQ took over in the interim
  • A distributor or reseller controls the customer relationships and growth has stopped — and you can't see why
  • The board approved LatAm as a priority and now expects a revenue number the current setup can't deliver
  • A new product or segment is being taken to the region — the home-market process doesn't apply and there is no regional GTM playbook
  • LatAm grew through inbound, but the operation never got a structure — a side project inside a company that now depends on it

Size is not the criterion

The commercial engine makes sense whenever the block is GTM and execution in the region — whether LatAm is your first international market or one of many. The criterion is the nature of the problem and the commitment behind the region, not your company's revenue.

5. How It Works Inside BOT — From Validation to Transfer

The commercial work follows the four phases of the Build-Operate-Transfer model. Each phase ends with a clear milestone — not a report, not a recommendation.

Phase 01 Validate & Align Weeks 1 → 6
Phases 02–03 Build & Drive Revenue Month 2 → 36

Typical engagement: 4 years, ending in a clean transfer to your team

Phase 01 — Validate & Align · Weeks 1 to 6

  • Market assessment and demand validation for your specific product and ICP in the target countries
  • Revenue quantification: what LatAm is worth for your product, and what it will take to capture it
  • Win-pattern analysis of your home-market deals, to separate what transfers to the region from what has to be rebuilt
  • Assessment of the sales model: which GTM motion fits local buyers (direct, channel, ABS, hybrid), given your ICP and ACV
  • KPIs and 3-year targets agreed with your leadership, with full governance transparency
  • Partnership terms formalized — including how and when the transfer happens

Phase 01 Deliverable

A validated LatAm case: demand confirmed for your ICP, revenue quantified, GTM motion defined, KPIs and 3-year targets agreed. You know exactly what LatAm is worth — and what it will take.

Phases 02–03 — Build the Foundation and Drive Revenue · Months 2 to 36

Starts immediately after Phase 01. While the entity is registered, the commercial operation is built and runs under our entity, so revenue work doesn't wait for paperwork.

  • ICP and segmentation for the region: target countries and cities, account tiers, qualification criteria
  • GTM motion implemented: direct, channel program or hybrid — each motion requires distinct processes, metrics and enablement
  • Sales process: defined stages, exit criteria, discovery templates adapted to local buying committees
  • Messaging and pricing: value props by persona in Portuguese and Spanish, battle cards, localized pricing
  • First hires from our vetted local network: country manager, enterprise AEs, technical staff
  • RevOps and rituals: weekly forecast, pipeline review, deal review, structured 1:1s
  • Dashboards: real-time visibility for the region, reported to HQ in your format

Milestones

A local team on the ground before Month 6. First revenue within 12 months. A regional GTM playbook running in production: documented ICP, motion, stage criteria, rituals and dashboards.

8. The 7 Errors That Block LatAm Growth

1. Scaling before having process

Hiring more local salespeople before having a regional playbook, a clear ICP, and a documented process multiplies chaos. Architecture first, then scale.

2. Confusing activity with results

A full pipeline with a low win rate is worse than a smaller pipeline with high quality. Vanity metrics hide the real problem — and from HQ they are even harder to see through.

3. HQ as permanent bottleneck

When pricing, discounts and every non-standard deal need approval from headquarters, the regional team can't move at the speed local buyers expect. Decision rights have to be designed, not improvised.

4. ICP too broad

"Our product works for any company in Latin America" is the phrase that has cost international companies the most money in the region. Vague ICP = generic messaging = high CAC = long cycle.

5. Not knowing why you lose deals

Without structured win/loss analysis, you make decisions based on guesswork. Each lost deal without diagnosis is wasted money and learning.

6. Metrics without an owner

If in your next board meeting you can't answer the region's CAC, win rate by stage, and NRR in under 5 minutes, you have a RevOps problem — not a sales problem.

7. Expecting quick magic

A new market takes longer than a turnaround at home. Plan for a local team before Month 6 and first revenue within 12 months. Anyone promising LatAm revenue in 90 days is selling an illusion.

9. Checklist: Is Your Company Ready for LatAm?

Answer honestly before any decision:

  • Do we have proven product-market fit at home (customers paying and renewing)?
  • Is LatAm a stated leadership priority, with budget approved?
  • Is our budget planned for a multi-year build, not a short pilot?
  • Do we win deals through a direct, considered B2B sale?
  • Do we already have some presence in the region — customers, partners or inbound demand?
  • Can we support customers in the region (CS, implementation, language)?
  • Do we understand the compliance exposure of our product in the target countries?
  • Are we willing to delegate commercial decisions to the regional operation?
  • Do we have patience for a 12-month path to first revenue?

If you answered "yes" to 7+ items, your company is the right fit. Size is not the criterion — commitment to the region and the nature of the problem are.

International B2B tech companies entering the region face specific challenges: tax complexity that impacts pricing and margin (Brazil alone requires 1,501 hours a year in tax compliance), longer sales cycles due to multiple stakeholders, scarcity of executives with real experience scaling B2B tech in the region, and a high cost of capital that makes local buyers demanding about ROI.

Timing matters: After the 2022-2024 valley of death, investors are prioritizing efficient growth over growth at any cost. A LatAm operation has to show a path to payback from the start — which is why the commercial engine is built before the team is scaled, not after.
